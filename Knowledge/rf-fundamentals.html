RF fundamentals

The physics behind every radio, SATCOM terminal and Wi-Fi link: spectrum, dB math, antennas, matching and line of sight.

Wavelength: λ (m) = 300 ÷ f (MHz). So a half-wave dipole at 30 MHz is about 5 m long (in practice ~95% of that, because of end effects).

  • dBm = 10·log10(mW). dBW = dBm − 30.
  • dBi = gain vs an isotropic radiator. dBd = gain vs a dipole. dBi = dBd + 2.15.
  • Chain math: transmitter power (dBm) − cable loss + antenna gain = EIRP.

Antennas

  • Gain is focus, not added power. A high-gain antenna puts more energy in some directions by taking it from others.
  • Omnidirectional (whips, verticals): 360° horizontal coverage. Easy to set up, but it radiates everywhere, which makes it easier to detect and to jam.
  • Directional (Yagi, log-periodic, panel, dish): higher gain, narrower beam, less energy where you don't want it. Must be aimed.
  • Polarization must match between ends. Vertical-to-horizontal mismatch can cost 20 dB or more on line-of-sight links.
  • Height usually helps more than power on VHF/UHF line-of-sight links.

High VSWR usually means a damaged or wet connector or cable, an antenna cut or tuned for a different frequency, or a missing ground/counterpoise.

Line of sight & Fresnel zone

Radio horizon (km) ≈ 4.12 × (√h1 + √h2)        h in metres, 4/3-earth
First Fresnel radius (m) = 17.32 × √( d1·d2 / (f_GHz · D) )   d in km

Keep at least 60% of the first Fresnel zone clear at the worst point (usually mid-path). Visual line of sight isn't enough on longer microwave and UHF shots.

Emissions awareness

Every transmission can be detected and direction-found. The basic levers: transmit only what you need, at the lowest power that closes the link, through the most directional antenna practical, using terrain to mask the signal from likely threat directions.
