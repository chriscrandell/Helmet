SATCOM fundamentals

Orbits, frequency bands, polarization and what degrades a link. This is the 100/200-level theory everything else on this shelf builds on.

Orbits

OrbitAltitudeCharacteristics
LEO~160–2,000 kmLow latency (tens of ms). Satellites move fast across the sky, so you need large constellations and tracking or phased-array terminals.
MEO2,000–35,786 kmMid latency. GPS sits here (~20,200 km), as do some broadband constellations.
GEO35,786 km above the equatorFixed point in the sky, so you can use fixed pointing. One hop ground→sat→ground ≈ 240+ ms, and a round trip ≈ 500+ ms. Poor coverage above ~70–75° latitude.
HEO (e.g. Molniya)EllipticalLong dwell over high latitudes, where GEO is weak.

GEO latency matters: TCP throughput over a high-latency path suffers unless you use acceleration or tuned window sizes. Interactive apps feel it too.

Frequency bands

BandRange (IEEE)Typical SATCOM use
UHF300 MHz – 1 GHzMilitary narrowband tactical SATCOM (manpack, on-the-move). Foliage-tolerant, low data rate, congested.
L1 – 2 GHzMobile satellite services (handhelds, small terminals). Rain-tolerant.
S2 – 4 GHzMobile services, telemetry.
C4 – 8 GHzCommercial trunking. Uplink ~5.9–6.4 GHz, downlink ~3.7–4.2 GHz. Very rain-tolerant, but needs big dishes.
X8 – 12 GHzMilitary wideband (e.g. WGS). Uplink ~7.9–8.4 GHz, downlink ~7.25–7.75 GHz. Good rain performance.
Ku12 – 18 GHzCommercial VSAT / broadband. Uplink ~14 GHz, downlink ~11–12 GHz. Moderate rain fade.
K / Ka18 – 27 / 27 – 40 GHzHigh-throughput spot beams, small terminals. Military Ka uplink ~30–31 GHz, downlink ~20.2–21.2 GHz. Significant rain fade.

Rule of thumb: higher frequency → more gain from the same dish and more bandwidth, but more rain fade and tighter pointing.

Polarization

  • Linear (horizontal/vertical): common on C and Ku. Needs a polarization (skew) adjustment that depends on your location relative to the satellite.
  • Circular (RHCP/LHCP): common on X, Ka, L and UHF. No skew adjustment.
  • Cross-pol isolation: transmitting on the wrong polarization, or with a poorly set skew, interferes with other users. That's why commissioning includes a cross-pol check with the satellite operator.

What degrades a link

  • Free-space path loss: huge (~200 dB to GEO) but predictable. See the link budget.
  • Rain fade: grows sharply above ~10 GHz, so Ku and especially Ka need fade margin or adaptive coding/modulation.
  • Atmospheric absorption and scintillation: worse at low elevation angles, where the signal travels through more atmosphere.
  • Pointing error: off-axis loss rises fast with dish size and frequency.
  • Interference: adjacent satellites, cross-pol, terrestrial emitters, and your own site's other transmitters.
  • Sun outage: twice a year around the equinoxes, the sun sits behind a GEO satellite from your site for a few minutes a day.
  • Blockage: terrain, buildings, vehicles. Check the look angle against the horizon.

Key terms

EIRPEffective isotropic radiated power: transmit power + antenna gain − line losses (dBW).
G/TReceive figure of merit: antenna gain ÷ system noise temperature (dB/K). Higher is better.
C/N, C/N₀Carrier-to-noise ratio, and carrier-to-noise density (per Hz).
Eb/N₀Energy per bit ÷ noise density. The modem needs a minimum value for a given modulation and coding (MODCOD).
Transponder / beamThe satellite's channel / coverage area. Spot beams trade coverage for gain.
BUC / LNBBlock upconverter (transmit amplifier + upconversion) / low-noise block downconverter (receive).
